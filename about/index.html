About

Linyu Xiang

Linyu Xiang is a landscape architect and researcher working on the measurement of urban perception. They hold a Master of Landscape Architecture from the University of Hong Kong and a B.Eng. in Landscape Architecture from Chongqing University.

Their research begins with a simple but stubborn question: when a vision-language model scores a street as green, safe, or lively, what part of that score comes from the image, and what part comes from what the model already assumes about the place? To answer it, they combine controlled input perturbations with layer-wise readouts of open-weight models, then map the resulting internal difference across the street network. In a 5,000-point study of Shenzhen, this approach showed that source reliance is spatially structured and associated with urban form — evidence that output accuracy alone cannot reveal where a model is measuring and where it is guessing. The paper has been submitted to Sustainable Cities and Society.

They are now extending the question from model attribution to measurement validity: which view of a city — street level, window level, aerial, or rendered — can legitimately stand for which construct, and under what conditions? A second strand translates audited perception measures into a perceptual layer for urban indices, so that a number entering a livability or beauty index carries its provenance and its limits.

Before this, they trained as a landscape architect. Their design work includes a Gold Award–winning thesis on Feng Shui knowledge and landscape ecology, an urban regeneration plan for the historic Shan Tang area in Suzhou, and a built installation from reclaimed construction waste in Kunming. They have taught landscape studio at HKU and worked on healthy-community measurement at the UEHH Lab.

They work in English, Mandarin, and Cantonese, and are based between Hong Kong and Shenzhen.

Approach

01

Measure the instrument

A model is not a transparent window. Before using its output as an indicator, make its assumptions observable.

02

Keep the drawing

A drawing is a way to test whether an argument about a place actually holds together. Design training is still part of the method.

03

Make it reproducible

If a colleague cannot recompute it, it is not yet a measurement. Prefer a plain script and a stated range to a polished dashboard.
